What is the Blue Team?

Foundation

In cybersecurity, the world is divided into two main sides: the Red Team (attackers / offensive) and the Blue Team (defenders / defensive). Think of it like a football match — the red team tries to score (break in), and the blue team tries to stop them.

💡 Simple Definition: The Blue Team is the group of security professionals whose job is to detect, defend, and respond to cyber attacks. They protect the organization's systems, networks, and data from being compromised.
Red vs Blue — Attack & Defense Model

Why Blue Team Matters

While offensive security (red team, ethical hacking) gets a lot of the spotlight, blue team roles represent over 65% of all cybersecurity jobs. Every organization — bank, hospital, government, startup — needs people who can monitor and defend their systems 24/7.

What is a Security Operations Center (SOC)?

Core Concept

A Security Operations Center (SOC) is the nerve center of an organization's cybersecurity operations. It's a centralized team — and often a physical room full of screens — where security analysts work 24/7/365 to monitor, detect, investigate, and respond to cyber threats.

🏢 Real-World Analogy: Think of the SOC like a hospital emergency room. Just as the ER never closes and doctors monitor patients constantly, the SOC never sleeps and analysts monitor digital "vital signs" of the organization around the clock.
How a SOC Works — The Alert Lifecycle

What Does the SOC Monitor?

  • Network traffic — packets flowing in and out of the organization
  • Endpoint activity — what programs are running on company computers
  • Authentication logs — who logged in, from where, when
  • Email security — phishing attempts, malicious attachments
  • Cloud environments — AWS, Azure, GCP activity
  • Application logs — web servers, databases, APIs
  • Vulnerability feeds — new CVEs that could affect the org
⚠️ Scale Challenge: Enterprise SOCs receive hundreds to thousands of alerts per day. Not every alert is a real threat — around 50-70% are false positives. A Tier 1 analyst's core job is rapidly determining which alerts need immediate action.
03

SOC Analyst Tiers — Tier 1, 2, and 3

Career

The SOC is organized into three tiers based on experience, responsibility, and the complexity of work. Think of it like a hospital: junior doctors handle common cases, senior doctors handle complex ones, and specialists handle the rarest and most critical situations.

T1
Entry Level · 0–2 yrs

🟢 Tier 1 Analyst

The first responder. You're the security team's "eyes" — watching dashboards and triaging every incoming alert.

  • Monitor SIEM dashboards 24/7
  • Triage alerts — real or false positive?
  • Create and assign incident tickets
  • Document findings clearly
  • Escalate confirmed incidents to Tier 2
  • Apply basic playbook responses
Salary: $55,000–$80,000 (US)
Certs: Security+, CySA+
T2
Mid Level · 2–5 yrs

🔵 Tier 2 Analyst

The deep diver. You take escalated incidents from Tier 1 and perform thorough investigation, asking "What exactly happened? How far did it spread?"

  • Deep-dive incident investigation
  • Correlate events across multiple systems
  • Build incident timelines
  • Authorized to isolate endpoints / block IPs
  • Basic malware triage (strings, behavior)
  • Tune SIEM rules to reduce false positives
  • Write incident reports for leadership
Salary: $80,000–$110,000 (US)
Certs: GCIH, ECIH, CySA+
T3
Expert · 5+ yrs

🟣 Tier 3 Analyst

The hunter and architect. You don't wait for alerts — you proactively hunt threats that have evaded all automated detection.

  • Proactive threat hunting
  • Advanced forensic investigations
  • Handle major incidents (ransomware, APTs)
  • Build custom SIEM detection rules
  • Write SOAR playbooks & automation
  • Vulnerability research & intelligence
  • Mentor Tier 1 & 2 analysts
Salary: $110,000–$160,000+ (US)
Certs: GCIA, OSCP, CISSP

Log Analysis — Reading the Digital Footprints

Core Skill

Logs are records of everything that happens on a computer system — who logged in, what files were accessed, which programs ran, and what network connections were made. Log analysis is the #1 daily skill of a SOC analyst.

🔍 Why Logs Matter: Every attacker leaves traces. When a hacker breaks into a system, they generate logs — failed login attempts, new user creation, suspicious process execution. The SOC analyst's job is to find these traces before the damage escalates.

Types of Logs a SOC Analyst Reads

1. Windows Event Logs

Windows keeps a detailed record of everything using Event IDs. Here are the most critical ones every blue teamer must memorize:

Event IDMeaningWhy It Matters
4624Successful loginTrack who logged in and when
4625Failed login attemptBrute force attacks look like many 4625s
4648Login with explicit credentialsLateral movement indicator
4672Admin privileges assignedPrivilege escalation warning
4688New process createdMalware execution detection
4698Scheduled task createdPersistence mechanism
4720New user account createdBackdoor account creation
7045New service installedMalware installs itself as a service

2. Syslog (Linux / Unix)

Linux systems use Syslog to record events. Key log files to know:

# Key Linux log locations
/var/log/auth.log      # SSH logins, sudo usage, authentication
/var/log/syslog        # General system events
/var/log/messages      # System messages (RHEL/CentOS)
/var/log/kern.log      # Kernel messages
/var/log/apache2/      # Web server access and error logs
/var/log/cron.log      # Scheduled jobs (cron) — persistence check

# Example: Check failed SSH logins
grep "Failed password" /var/log/auth.log

# Example: Find successful sudo usage
grep "sudo" /var/log/auth.log | grep "COMMAND"

3. Network / Firewall Logs

Firewall logs record every connection attempt — allowed or blocked. Key things to look for:

  • Outbound traffic to unusual IPs — possible C2 (Command & Control) communication
  • Port scanning activity — many connection attempts to different ports from one IP
  • Large data transfers (exfiltration) — unusually large outbound data
  • Traffic on non-standard ports — malware sometimes uses weird ports to hide
  • DNS queries to suspicious domains — malware phoning home via DNS
📌 Beginner Tip — What Makes a Log Entry Suspicious?
Ask yourself three questions: (1) Is the time unusual? (3am login from CEO?) (2) Is the location unusual? (login from Russia for an India-based user?) (3) Is the action unusual? (normal employee suddenly creating new admin accounts?)

Incident Response — The PICERL Lifecycle

Framework

When a real attack is confirmed, the SOC follows a structured Incident Response (IR) plan. The most widely used framework is PICERL, developed by the SANS Institute. Each letter is a phase of the response.

🎯 Why PICERL Matters: Without a structured plan, responding to an attack becomes chaotic. PICERL gives every team member a clear role and sequence to follow, transforming panic into a controlled, effective response.
P
Preparation

Build your defenses before an attack. Train staff, write playbooks, set up tools, run drills.

I
Identification

Detect & confirm: Is this a real incident? What systems are affected? What's the scope?

C
Containment

Stop the spread. Isolate infected machines, block malicious IPs, disable compromised accounts.

E
Eradication

Fully remove the threat. Delete malware, patch vulnerabilities, re-image infected systems.

R
Recovery

Restore systems to normal. Verify clean backups, re-enable services, monitor for recurrence.

L
Lessons Learned

Post-incident review. What failed? What worked? Update playbooks. Improve detection rules.

Real-World Ransomware Walkthrough Using PICERL

Let's say a company gets hit by ransomware. Here's exactly how PICERL plays out:

P

Preparation (done in advance)

IR playbook for ransomware exists. Offline backups taken weekly. Staff trained not to click suspicious emails. EDR and SIEM in place. Incident response team contacts are documented.

I

Identification — 9:14 AM

SIEM alerts on rapid file encryption across FILESERVER-01. User calls helpdesk: "All my files say .locked extension." Tier 1 confirms: ransomware. Files show YOUR_FILES_ARE_ENCRYPTED.txt. Incident declared.

C

Containment — 9:22 AM

EDR used to isolate all infected endpoints from the network. VLAN blocked. Shared drives unmounted remotely. Domain Controller checked — clean. Firewall blocks all outbound C2 IPs identified in malware IOCs.

E

Eradication — 11:00 AM

Malware sample sent to sandbox analysis. Phishing email identified as entry point. 12 affected machines re-imaged from clean images. Patch for exploited vulnerability applied. All user passwords reset.

R

Recovery — 2:00 PM

Clean backups from 3 days ago restored (some data lost but manageable). Systems scanned and verified clean. Services brought back online one by one. 48-hour enhanced monitoring initiated.

L

Lessons Learned — Next Week

Post-incident review: phishing email bypassed email filter due to zero-day domain. Email security vendor notified. New SIEM rule created to detect rapid file rename activity. Staff phishing training updated. Playbook improved.

✅ Key Insight: The Lessons Learned phase is the most often skipped — but it's the most valuable. Every incident makes your SOC stronger if you learn from it and update your defenses.

MITRE ATT&CK Framework

Intelligence

MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) is essentially a giant library of how hackers attack. It's maintained by MITRE Corporation and is based on real-world observations of actual attacks. Every SOC analyst uses it.

💡 Simple Analogy: Imagine you're a police detective. ATT&CK is your criminal database — it tells you: "Drug dealers use these routes. Fraudsters use these methods. Hackers use these techniques." It gives defenders a common language to describe and communicate about threats.

The Three Layers: Tactics → Techniques → Procedures

🎯 Tactics

WHY — The attacker's goal. What are they trying to achieve at this step?

Example: "Initial Access" — The attacker wants to get their first foothold into the network.

⚙️ Techniques

HOW — The specific method used to achieve the tactic.

Example: "Spearphishing Attachment" (T1566.001) — Sending a malicious email with an infected file attached.

📋 Procedures

SPECIFICALLY HOW — The exact implementation by a real threat actor.

Example: "APT28 sent a .docx file with a macro that downloads Cobalt Strike beacon when opened."

🔍 Threat Hunting — Don't Wait for Alerts

Advanced

Reactive security (waiting for alerts) catches known threats. But advanced attackers often operate silently for weeks or months before detection — this is called "dwell time." The average dwell time globally is ~200 days. Threat hunting is the solution.

⚠️ The Problem with Reactive-Only Defense: If a skilled attacker uses only legitimate Windows tools (living off the land), custom malware with no known signatures, and moves slowly — traditional SIEM rules may never fire an alert. They're already inside.

What is Threat Hunting?

Threat hunting is the proactive search for attackers who have already bypassed your defenses. Instead of waiting for an alert, a threat hunter asks: "Given what I know about how attackers operate, what evidence would they leave? Let me go look for it."

1

Create a Hypothesis

Based on threat intelligence, ATT&CK, or gut feeling: "I think an attacker may be using scheduled tasks for persistence on our servers."

2

Collect & Search Data

Pull logs from SIEM for all scheduled tasks created in the last 30 days. Filter for ones created by non-admin accounts or outside business hours.

3

Investigate Anomalies

Found a scheduled task svchost32.exe running every 5 minutes created at 3am by a standard user account. That's suspicious — investigate deeper.

4

Respond or Tune

If real threat: incident response. If not: tune your SIEM to automatically alert on this pattern next time. Every hunt makes your detection better.

  • CrowdStrike Falcon — Industry-leading EDR. Detects malware behavior in real time.

  • Wireshark — Capture and analyze network packets. See exactly what traffic flows.

Threat Intelligence

  • VirusTotal — Check if a file/IP/domain is known malicious. Free.
  • AbuseIPDB — Community database of malicious IP addresses. Free.
  • MISP — Open-source threat intelligence sharing platform.
  • OTX AlienVault — Free open threat exchange — IOCs shared by the community.
